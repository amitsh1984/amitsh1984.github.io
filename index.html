Craft + AI

Traditional methods and AI, each where it works best

I wrote documentation for 14 years before generative AI arrived. I use AI where it saves time without risking accuracy, and keep people in charge of the steps that need product knowledge and judgment.

StepStageTraditional practiceWhere AI helps
01

Research

Writer-led
TraditionalSME interviews, product demos, user stories, and hands-on use of the feature.
AISummarize long tickets and comment threads into a starting brief.
02

First draft

AI-assisted
TraditionalWriters rewrite the draft in the house voice and fill gaps from the product.
AILLM-based workflows draft release notes and help updates from shipped user stories: 5 days down to 1–2.
03

Validation

Writer-led
TraditionalEvery step tested in the product. Screenshots and field names checked against the build.
AINone. Accuracy is checked by a person.
04

Editorial review

Shared
TraditionalMy final redline against the Microsoft Manual of Style, CLM terminology, and publication readiness.
AIA first-pass review that flags style, terminology, and internal references before I read.
05

Publish & localize

Shared
TraditionalPublishing in Document360, version control, and German sign-off by a reviewer.
AISegment-by-segment accuracy checks of German pages against the English source.

My guardrails: AI never publishes. Every AI draft is built from a named source, anything inferred is flagged for the product owner, and a writer signs off before content goes live.

Selected work

Programs I built and run

Platform migrationRoboHelp 11 → Document360

Moving the entire help center to a modern knowledge base

Problem
Help content lived in RoboHelp 11, a desktop authoring tool. Publishing was slow, writers couldn't collaborate, and customers had no good web search.
Action
Spearheaded the migration of 2,600+ articles to Document360 and restructured them into separate user and administrator portals, a dedicated API and integration section, and English and German versions.
Result
A web-native help center that the team keeps current: 68% of user and administrator pages were refreshed in the last 12 months.
Self-serviceHelp center adoption

Getting customers to answer their own questions

Problem
A help center only reduces support load if customers use it. After the migration, the help manual had to become the first place customers look, not the last.
Action
Task-based articles with one consistent structure across portals, plus a monthly review that turns support tickets caused by documentation gaps into new content.
Result
Monthly views grew 25× within three months of launch, and the first half of 2026 averaged 30% more views a month than the second half of 2025. 82% of views are full reads. Over the same period, tickets classified as functional gaps or invalid bugs (users reporting expected behavior as a defect) fell 20% year on year while overall ticket volume stayed flat.

AI-assisted release docsEvery release cycle

Release documentation drafted in a day, not a week

Problem
Release notes were assembled by hand from tickets every cycle, and a first draft took five working days.
Action
Built LLM-based drafting workflows that turn shipped user stories into first drafts of release notes, release previews, help-manual updates, and website feature pages. Added a house style for release notes, an AI first-pass review returned as a redline, and an interactive sign-off email that product managers confirm per feature.
Result
First drafts in 1–2 working days instead of 5. Writers still validate every draft against the product and sign off before anything is published.
Digital adoptionWalkMe → Whatfix

In-app guidance that cut training costs and earned revenue

Problem
New users needed training to get started, and how-to questions turned into support tickets.
Action
Introduced WalkMe to move user onboarding and feature adoption into the product. Later led the switch to Whatfix, migrated the in-app content, and onboarded custom Whatfix accounts for clients, gathering their requirements directly.
Result
Lower training costs, fewer how-to tickets, and around 15 custom Whatfix client accounts that contributed to company revenue.
